\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter establishes the concepts and terminology used throughout the
thesis and positions CD4AI in relation to existing work. It introduces
continuous delivery and deployment pipelines
(Section~\ref{sec:background-delivery}), the applications built with foundation
models considered in this study (Section~\ref{sec:background-ai}), the testing
and evaluation terminology adopted in later chapters
(Section~\ref{sec:background-evaluation}), and the structure of software
patterns (Section~\ref{sec:background-patterns}). It then reviews related
approaches in AI engineering, observability, and evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:background-related-work}).

\section{Continuous Delivery and Feedback from Use}
\label{sec:background-delivery}

Continuous integration (CI) is the practice of integrating code changes
frequently and verifying each integration through an automated build and test
process~\parencite{Fowler:ContinuousIntegration:2024}. Continuous delivery (CD)
extends this automation through a \emph{deployment pipeline} so that the
software is kept in a releasable state~\parencite{Humble:ContinuousDelivery:2010}.
As a proposed change moves through the pipeline, automated checks provide
feedback to developers and act as quality gates: if a required check fails, the
pipeline prevents the candidate from advancing until the team investigates and
resolves the issue~\parencite{Humble:ContinuousDelivery:2010}. Keeping the
software releasable is distinct from automatically deploying every change to
production; release remains a policy decision.

Pre-deployment checks provide evidence within the scope of their inputs,
criteria, and execution conditions; they do not cover every situation that may
arise during use. Post-deployment monitoring and user feedback therefore
complement pre-deployment testing by exposing unanticipated problems in
production. In machine learning systems, \textcite{Sato:ContinuousDelivery4ML:2019}
describe how Continuous Delivery for Machine Learning (CD4ML) combines pipeline
testing with production monitoring so that observed production data can be fed
back into subsequent model development. Chapter~\ref{chap:cd4ai} builds on
these continuous delivery principles to connect production monitoring and
curation to the evaluation suites run in the delivery pipeline.

\section{AI-Enabled Systems and Foundation Models}
\label{sec:background-ai}

\textcite{Martinez:SE4AI:2022} define \emph{AI-based systems}---referred to in
this thesis as \emph{AI-enabled systems}---as software systems whose
functionality is enabled by at least one artificial intelligence component.
While some AI-enabled systems use task-specific models trained for a single
function, this thesis focuses on applications built around \emph{foundation
models}: models trained on broad data at scale, typically through
self-supervision, that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream
tasks~\parencite{Bommasani:FoundationModels:2021}. Pretraining alone does not
make a model a foundation model; the term emphasizes broad training and
adaptability as a shared basis across tasks.

In applications built with language foundation models, behavior depends not
only on the model itself, but also on the surrounding application components,
which may include prompts, information retrieved from external sources to supply
context (retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG), external tools, and
orchestration logic~\parencite{Schluntz:BuildingEffectiveAgents:2024}. Teams
can therefore alter application behavior by modifying these components without
retraining the underlying model.

These applications can admit multiple valid outputs for the same input, so
exact matching against a single reference string is not always sufficient.
Their behavior can also vary across executions under unchanged conditions,
requiring attention to run-to-run variation when interpreting evaluation
results~\parencite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026}.

\section{Testing and Evaluation Terminology}
\label{sec:background-evaluation}

The terms \emph{test} and \emph{evaluation} overlap in AI engineering practice.
For example, \textcite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026} describe an evaluation,
often abbreviated to \emph{eval}, as a test that supplies an input to an AI
system and assesses its output using grading logic. The terminology below
establishes a convention for this thesis rather than a universal distinction
between the two terms.

\subsection{Evaluations, Cases, and Tests}

In this thesis, evaluations assess selected aspects of an AI-enabled
application's behavior, which may depend on models, prompts, retrieval, tools,
and application logic.

\begin{description}
  \item[Evaluation.] The assessment of application behavior against specified
    criteria. Results may include scores, pass/fail judgments, and summaries
    across cases or repeated executions.
  \item[Evaluation case.] An input or scenario retained for later evaluation,
    together with the relevant context, the expected behavior, and the criteria
    that the application's behavior must satisfy. The expected behavior need not
    be a single reference answer.
  \item[Assessment method.] A procedure that assesses an application's output or
    behavior against a criterion and returns an assessment, such as a numerical
    score or a pass/fail result. For example, an LLM judge may assess whether an
    answer follows a refund policy. The criterion specifies the conditions being
    checked; the assessment method applies it. A delivery policy determines how
    the result affects deployment.
  \item[Test.] An executable check of expected behavior that may be used within
    an evaluation. Calling a check a test does not imply that the application
    or the assessment method is deterministic.
  \item[Evaluation suite.] A collection of cases used to assess selected
    behaviors or capabilities. A dataset may store cases, while executing an
    evaluation also requires an application, an execution procedure, and a
    means of assessing the results.
\end{description}

This thesis uses \emph{evaluation} as the general term for behavioral assessment
and retains \emph{testing} when discussing executable checks and software
testing practices. The choice of term does not by itself determine whether a
result should block deployment.

In the title of this thesis, \emph{continuous evaluation} refers to assessment
repeatedly integrated into the delivery and feedback cycle. It does not require
uninterrupted execution or a particular evaluation framework. The cycle
supports evaluation of improvements as well as detection of regressions.

\subsection{Known Limitations and Regressions}
\label{sec:background-limitations-regressions}

This thesis uses \emph{known limitation} to refer to a recognized gap between
the application's expected and observed behavior that remains unresolved.
A \emph{regression} is an unintended deterioration in previously established
behavior following a change. It may involve the reappearance of a fixed defect
or the disruption of other behavior that previously worked.

These concepts can overlap: an identified regression that remains unresolved
can itself be recorded as a known limitation. Knowing that a problem already
exists does not establish whether a proposed change preserves or worsens the
affected behavior.

\subsection{Confirmation, Capability, and Regression Evaluation}
\label{sec:background-evaluation-purpose}

Confirmation testing assesses whether a proposed fix resolves an identified
defect. Regression testing checks for adverse effects of changes on existing
behavior~\cite[Section~2.2.3]{ISTQB:FoundationSyllabus:2024}. These purposes
should be distinguished from the time at which a case is written: a test
intended for regression protection may be created before the defect is fixed,
and a newly written test may expose a regression that has already occurred.

In the context of agents, \textcite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026} distinguish
capability evaluations, which assess abilities that may still need improvement,
from regression evaluations, which check whether established capabilities are
preserved. They also describe how cases initially used to guide improvement
can later support regression evaluation as the system becomes capable of
handling them.

Accordingly, a case documenting an unresolved defect remains useful even if it
currently fails. It can record the desired behavior and help assess proposed
fixes. Once that behavior is established, the same case can help detect its
deterioration under later changes. A continuing failure on a known unresolved
defect does not, by itself, demonstrate a new regression. Retaining a case for
evaluation is also distinct from deciding whether its failure blocks delivery;
that decision depends on the application's delivery policy
(Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy}).

\section{Software Patterns}
\label{sec:background-patterns}

In software engineering, a \emph{pattern} documents a reusable solution to a
recurring problem that arises within a specific context, together with the
rationale for applying that solution~\parencite{Meszaros:PatternWriting:1997}.
Rather than prescribing a specific software tool or a single fixed
implementation, this thesis uses the pattern form to identify the core
responsibilities and relationships that address the problem, allowing teams to
adapt the concrete mechanisms to their own systems.

Drawing on the pattern elements described by
\textcite{Meszaros:PatternWriting:1997}, in which the pattern name, context,
problem, forces, and solution are mandatory elements and the remaining elements
are optional, this thesis uses the following concepts:
\begin{description}
  \item[Pattern name.] A concise name by which the problem--solution pairing
    can be referenced.
  \item[Context and problem.] The \emph{context} defines the circumstances and
    constraints under which the pattern applies, while the \emph{problem}
    states the specific difficulty to be resolved within that context. Pattern
    descriptions may also state the pattern's \emph{intent}, summarizing its
    purpose.
  \item[Forces.] The competing considerations, goals, and constraints that make
    the problem difficult and shape the choice of solution.
  \item[Solution.] The proposed arrangement of responsibilities and connections
    that resolves the problem. In this thesis, the solution is specified by
    distinguishing its required elements from the mechanisms left as
    implementation options.
  \item[Consequences.] The resulting context after applying the pattern,
    including both its expected benefits and the costs, trade-offs, or
    limitations it introduces.
  \item[Examples and known uses.] Examples illustrate how a pattern can
    operate; known uses document applications of the solution in existing
    systems.
\end{description}

Chapter~\ref{chap:cd4ai} presents CD4AI's context, problem, forces, intent,
solution, and a worked example. Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} then examines
an implementation built for this study alongside three documented tool
workflows, and Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} discusses the pattern's design
trade-offs, consequences, and limitations in light of those cases.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

The works discussed here address how observations of application behavior
inform development, how agents are observed and assessed, and how people
establish evaluation criteria. Selected through an exploratory search rather
than a systematic literature review, they include research papers,
architectural pattern catalogues, and practitioner guidance. The comparison
focuses on the connections between these activities, including where a work
addresses only part of the feedback loop described by CD4AI.

\subsection{Feedback Loops in AI Development and Delivery}
\label{sec:related-feedback}

Learning from deployed behavior is already part of continuous delivery
approaches for machine learning. \textcite{Sato:ContinuousDelivery4ML:2019}
describe CD4ML as a process for delivering changes to code, data, and models.
Their process combines testing and deployment with production monitoring;
collected data can be reviewed and curated into new training datasets.
CD4AI shares this connection between observations from use and subsequent
development. Its focus is the preparation of evaluation cases for assessing
application changes, which need not involve retraining a model. The difference
concerns how the retained data is used in the described process.

For foundation-model agents, \textcite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026} recommend
turning user-reported failures into evaluation tasks, defining clear success
criteria, and maintaining the suite as the application evolves. Their guidance
combines automated evaluation, production monitoring, and human review. This
closely resembles CD4AI's loop: observations identify situations to examine,
review establishes what should have happened, and retained cases support later
checks. The article presents practical guidance drawn from agent development,
rather than a single pattern specification.

The connection to delivery also requires deciding how evaluation results affect
a proposed change. For example, \textcite{Renggli:EaseMLCI:2019} present
ease.ml/ci, in which developers specify integration conditions, including
comparisons between models, together with error tolerances and reliability
requirements. This provides a concrete treatment of evaluation-based
integration decisions. Its statistical procedure is distinct from CD4AI's
organization of the feedback loop, which leaves the assessment method and
delivery policy to the implementation.

\subsection{Patterns and Observability for Agents}
\label{sec:related-agent-patterns}

Pattern literature provides another perspective on these activities.
\textcite{Heiland:AIPatterns:2023} distinguish newly proposed AI patterns from
adaptations of established patterns and include process patterns alongside
architectural and deployment patterns. This distinction is relevant to CD4AI:
a pattern description may organize existing practices without introducing
each practice individually.

Within their catalogue for foundation-model agents,
\textcite{Liu:AgentPatterns:2024} describe the \emph{Agent Evaluator} pattern.
It assesses agents during development and operation using scenario-specific
requirements, metrics, expected outputs, and test cases. Evaluation results
can inform changes to both the agent and the evaluator. DeepEval is listed
among its known uses, connecting this pattern to one of the cases examined in
Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies}.

Agent Evaluator therefore overlaps with CD4AI in its use of assessment to guide
adaptation. The distinction is one of emphasis: its solution describes an
evaluation component and the feedback it produces, while CD4AI follows
observations from use through review and case preparation into evaluations of
subsequent application versions. The patterns can be complementary; an
evaluation component does not by itself explain how production interactions
are selected and retained as cases.

Observability supplies information needed for that process.
\textcite{Dong:AgentOps:2024} map AgentOps tools and propose a taxonomy of
agent artifacts and associated trace data. Their analysis covers evaluation,
user feedback, monitoring, and tracing. Feedback can be attached to execution
traces, while inspection of individual steps helps investigate unexpected
behavior. This gives a more detailed account of the evidence that can support
CD4AI's monitoring stage.

The comparison also clarifies the role of curation. Recording a trace or
attaching feedback makes an interaction available for investigation. Preparing
it for later evaluation additionally requires deciding what behavior to expect
and how to assess it. CD4AI makes this transition an explicit part of the loop,
without prescribing a particular tracing architecture.

\subsection{Human Review and the Construction of Evaluation Criteria}
\label{sec:related-curation}

Review is more than assigning labels using criteria that are already settled.
\textcite{Shankar:Validators:2024} study EvalGen, an interface that combines
human grading with assistance in generating evaluation criteria and their
implementations. Their user study identifies \emph{criteria drift}: examining
outputs can lead people to add criteria or reinterpret existing ones. Thus,
observing behavior and specifying how it should be assessed can influence each
other.

For CD4AI, this finding supports treating curation as a substantive development
activity. A selected interaction may reveal an expectation that was previously
implicit or ambiguous. Review must resolve that expectation sufficiently to
prepare a useful case. EvalGen focuses on aligning evaluators with human
judgments; it does not establish the operation or effectiveness of CD4AI's
complete delivery loop.

Practitioner guidance by \textcite{HusainShankar:EvalsFAQ:2026} connects this
review work more directly to production feedback. They describe collecting
traces, annotating problems, grouping related failures, and using the analysis
to decide what to evaluate. They also recommend adding representative examples
to a CI dataset when production monitoring reveals new failure patterns.
This corresponds closely to the transition from monitoring through curation
to evaluation. Their specific review techniques are possible implementations
of that transition, rather than requirements of CD4AI.

\subsection{Positioning CD4AI}
\label{sec:background-positioning}

Taken together, these works describe substantial parts of the process proposed
by CD4AI, and some explicitly connect production feedback to later evaluations.
The contribution pursued here is a pattern formulation that organizes these
connections around five required elements and examines their correspondence
with selected workflows. Specifically, the pattern requires evaluating proposed
changes against retained cases in the delivery pipeline, selecting interactions
from use for review, and curating selected problems into reusable cases with
expected behavior and assessment criteria. It further requires interpreting
results against evidence of previous behavior to distinguish continuing
limitations from potential regressions, and applying a delivery policy to
determine which results block delivery.

The comparison therefore concerns more than whether a system provides logging,
annotation, and evaluation features. It asks how material moves between those
activities: which observations are reviewed, how expectations become assessment
criteria, and how retained cases participate in later checks and delivery
decisions. Chapter~\ref{chap:cd4ai} presents the proposed structure, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} examines its correspondence with an
implementation and documented tool workflows. Neither the overlap with prior
work nor that correspondence establishes a measured improvement from adopting
the pattern.
