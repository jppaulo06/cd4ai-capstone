\chapter{The CD4AI Pattern}
\label{chap:cd4ai}

CD4AI is a continuous delivery pattern that addresses the following problem:
\enquote{How can teams turn failures observed during the use of AI-enabled
systems into evaluation cases that support subsequent changes?}
This chapter presents the pattern and its feedback loop.
Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} examines how its stages appear in selected
systems and documented workflows.

\section{Context, Problem, and Intent}
\label{sec:cd4ai-context}

% Context

Foundation models are trained on broad data at scale and can be adapted to
a range of downstream tasks~\cite{Bommasani:FoundationModels:2021}.
Applications built around language foundation models can change their behavior
through modifications to prompts, retrieved context, tools, and orchestration,
without necessarily retraining the underlying
model~\cite{Schluntz:BuildingEffectiveAgents:2024}.
By avoiding a model-training cycle for such changes, application teams can
shorten the implementation cycle and introduce behavioral changes within their
ordinary software development workflow.

In this setting, evaluating AI-dependent behavior becomes part of the
application team's delivery responsibilities, even when the team does not
develop or train the underlying model. A change that is quick to implement
may still require substantial evaluation. The resulting challenge is to assess
its effects on established behavior while incorporating failures discovered
during use into future evaluations.

% Optional motivation: add a concrete observation from the startup experience,
% distinguishing personal observations from claims about development practices

% Problem

Initial evaluations cannot be assumed to cover every interaction that will
emerge during use. Teams therefore need a process for identifying candidate
failures, reviewing them, and turning confirmed defects into useful evaluation
cases. Recording a failure is only the first step: the team must specify what
the application should do in that situation and use the resulting case to
evaluate future application versions.

A further difficulty arises when a suite contains both known unresolved
limitations and behavior that the team expects to preserve. An undifferentiated
failure report does not establish whether a proposed change introduced a
regression or whether a known limitation persists. The evaluation process must
retain enough context to interpret the result and support a delivery decision.

% Intent

CD4AI aims to support frequent delivery while preserving established behavior
by connecting evaluation, monitoring, and curation in a recurring feedback loop.
Although applications built around language foundation models motivate the
discussion here, the pattern is described in terms of these responsibilities,
rather than a particular model or evaluation framework. Its intent is to make
observed failures inform future validation, without guaranteeing that every
failure will be discovered or that a previously observed defect will never recur.

Following the terminology in Section~\ref{sec:background-evaluation}, these
cases can document unresolved limitations, support confirmation of fixes, and
help detect regressions. The evaluation stage includes these purposes;
creating a case does not imply that the corresponding defect has been fixed.

\section{Overview of the Feedback Loop}
\label{sec:cd4ai-overview}

CD4AI uses failures found during use to improve the evaluations run before
future deployments. The team repeats three stages:

\begin{description}
  \item[Evaluation.] Run the existing evaluation cases against a proposed
    change. Apply the team's delivery policy to decide whether the results
    permit deployment or require further work.
  \item[Monitoring.] Observe the deployed application and collect interactions
    that may reveal failures for the team to investigate.
  \item[Curation.] Review these interactions to identify actual problems.
    Turn selected problems into evaluation cases that specify the expected
    behavior and how to check it.
\end{description}

The same assessment tool may help flag interactions during monitoring,
review them during curation, and assess application versions during evaluation.
Its purpose differs at each stage.

Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-feedback-loop} summarizes the transitions between these
stages and the return of curated cases to evaluation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-feedback-loop}
  \caption{The CD4AI feedback loop. Evaluation provides evidence for delivery
    decisions; monitoring supplies candidate interactions for review;
    curation prepares evaluation cases for subsequent assessments.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-feedback-loop}
\end{figure}

The new cases are added to the evaluation suite and run against proposed fixes
and later changes, closing the loop. Selected failures discovered during use
therefore become part of what the team checks before future deployments.

Application changes, including defect fixes, are implemented through the team's
development process and assessed in the evaluation stage. Curation specifies cases
and expected behavior; it does not itself repair the application. The diagram
summarizes the flow of evaluation evidence rather than every development activity.

CD4AI relies on a delivery policy that determines how evaluation results inform
development and delivery decisions. Each implementation defines which outcomes prevent
deployment and how other results inform tracking and improvement. Cases may
track known limitations or protect established behavior against regressions.
Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy} discusses how teams can define and
apply this policy.

The following sections explain monitoring, curation, and evaluation in more
detail. Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-worked-example} then follows a hypothetical
refund problem through the stages and shows how the results inform a delivery
decision.

\section{Monitoring: Finding Candidate Problems}
\label{sec:cd4ai-monitoring}

Monitoring observes the deployed application and supplies candidate interactions
for review. Candidate selection and the context retained with each interaction
support the review performed during curation.

Monitoring selects interactions for curation using signals of possible
problems, sampling, or a combination of both. Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-candidate-selection}
illustrates this selection and the handoff to curation.

A \emph{signal} is an observable indication that an interaction may contain a
problem and deserves review. Examples include negative user feedback and an
automated evaluator identifying claims that the retrieved information does not
support~\parencite{MLflow:RetrievalGroundedness}. Neither confirms a defect:
feedback may express dissatisfaction with correct behavior, and the evaluator
may misjudge the answer. Signal design depends on the application; curation
determines whether a selected interaction reveals a defect.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-candidate-selection}
  \caption{Candidate selection within monitoring. Solid arrows show the flow
    of interactions toward curation; dashed arrows indicate what guides
    selection.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-candidate-selection}
\end{figure}

Precision and recall help describe selection
quality~\parencite[Section~8.3]{Manning:IR:2008}. For a defined population,
counting interactions rather than distinct defects:
\begin{gather*}
  \text{Precision} =
  \frac{\text{Number of selected problematic interactions}}
       {\text{Number of selected interactions}} \\[1ex]
  \text{Recall} =
  \frac{\text{Number of selected problematic interactions}}
       {\text{Number of problematic interactions}}
\end{gather*}
Low precision means a larger share of selected interactions consumes review
effort without revealing defects. Low recall means a larger share of problematic
interactions is missed. These measures help teams reason about selection
quality in relation to review capacity and application risks; CD4AI does not
require continuous measurement or prescribe numerical targets.

Sampling unflagged interactions can reveal problems that the signals missed,
helping more problematic interactions reach curation. This can improve the
recall of the overall selection process. Finding few defects in that sample
does not necessarily indicate poor selection, since its purpose is to explore
interactions that would otherwise go unreviewed.

Monitoring retains enough information for reviewers to reconstruct and assess
the relevant behavior. This includes the context available to the application
and the outputs it produced, such as inputs, conversation history, retrieved
information, tool responses, and tool calls.
Missing information can make review more difficult. For example, without the
tool description an agent received, a reviewer may be unable to assess whether
its tool call was appropriate. The retained context supports curation's
judgment about the selected interaction.

\section{Curation: Turning Observations into Evaluation Cases}
\label{sec:cd4ai-curation}

Curation has two activities: reviewing candidate interactions and preparing
reusable evaluation cases. Review determines whether an interaction reveals
a problem and clarifies the expected behavior. For problems selected for
inclusion, case preparation specifies the scenario, relevant context, and
assessment criteria used to evaluate future application versions.
Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-curation-activities} shows the connection between these
activities.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-curation-activities}
  \caption{Review and case preparation within curation. Only problems selected
    for inclusion proceed to case preparation.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-curation-activities}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Reviewing Candidate Interactions}

Review determines whether the observed behavior met the relevant requirements
or expectations. A monitoring signal alone does not establish that a defect
occurred. People, automated assessment, or a combination of both may perform
this review. CD4AI leaves that choice to the implementation.

Reviewing an interaction may require product or domain knowledge in addition
to engineering knowledge. The reviewer needs to understand the relevant
requirements, policies, and intended user experience to determine whether the
behavior is acceptable. For example, deciding whether an assistant should
grant a refund depends on the product's refund policy, not just on whether
the answer is coherent. Depending on the application, this review may involve
product specialists or domain experts alongside engineers.

An interaction may also expose a situation the team had not anticipated.
Existing requirements may already determine the expected behavior; otherwise,
the team needs to clarify what the application should do before preparing an
evaluation case. Review can therefore reveal gaps in expectations as well as
failures to meet existing ones. Once the expected behavior is sufficiently
clear, the team can decide whether to retain the interaction as a reusable case.

\subsection{Preparing Evaluation Cases}

For each interaction selected for inclusion, curation prepares a reusable case
with a scenario and assessment criteria. The scenario includes the input and
any context needed to exercise the observed problem, such as conversation
history, retrieved information, or tool state. The prepared case is retained
for use in subsequent evaluations.

Assessment criteria specify what the application must do for its response to
be considered acceptable. They need not require the response to match a
reference answer word for word. For example, several answers may correctly
explain why a purchase is ineligible for a refund. The criteria can check
whether the explanation correctly applies the refund policy, regardless of
the exact words used. Assessment methods include, for example, rule-based
checks on structured outputs and LLMs acting as judges that assess responses
against a rubric~\parencite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026}.

A case can be retained before its defect is fixed, allowing the team to track
the problem and assess proposed fixes. Whether a continuing failure blocks
deployment is determined by the delivery policy, as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy}.

\section{Evaluation: Assessing Changes and Supporting Delivery}
\label{sec:cd4ai-testing}

Evaluation exercises an application version against retained cases and
assesses its behavior using their criteria. The results help the team test
proposed fixes, track unresolved limitations, and check whether changes
preserve established behavior. Within the delivery pipeline, these results
inform whether a proposed change can proceed or requires further work.

\subsection{Interpreting Results}
\label{sec:cd4ai-result-interpretation}

A failing evaluation does not by itself show whether a proposed change
introduced a problem or whether an existing limitation remains unresolved.
Determining whether the change introduced the problem requires evidence about
the application's previous behavior. As
Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-result-interpretation} shows, comparing a case's results on
the current version with those on the proposed version can help distinguish
these situations. When evidence about previous behavior is unavailable,
the team may identify a defect without yet determining whether it is a
regression.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-result-interpretation}
  \caption{Interpreting a case by comparing its results on the current and
    proposed versions. When a case runs repeatedly, pass and fail summarize
    each version's runs, for example as pass rates.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-result-interpretation}
\end{figure}

Distinguishing continuing limitations from potential regressions can help teams
identify new failures despite existing ones and keep unresolved limitations
visible.

To isolate the effects of an application change, comparisons should use the
same cases and assessment criteria, with other execution conditions held
constant as far as possible. Otherwise, a difference may come from the
evaluation itself; for example, a stricter rubric used by an LLM judge may
produce lower scores even when the application's behavior has not changed.
Differences can also arise from changes in external dependencies: an update to
the underlying model, for instance, may alter the application's behavior even
when its code and prompts remain unchanged.

Even when the application version and evaluation conditions remain unchanged,
probabilistic generation can produce different responses across runs. A case
may therefore reproduce the conditions of a failure without reproducing the
failure every time. To account for
variability in the application or its evaluators, evaluation may exercise the
same case multiple times to assess how consistently the application meets the
expected behavior~\parencite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026}. A single passing
run does not establish that an intermittent defect has been resolved.

When results vary across runs, statistical comparisons can help determine
whether an observed deterioration provides evidence of a regression.
One possible implementation uses Fisher's exact
test~\parencite{Fisher:DesignOfExperiments:1935, SciPy:FisherExact}. For each
case, it compares how often each version passed across its runs, testing
specifically for a lower pass rate in the proposed version (a one-sided test).
Cases whose p-value falls below a chosen threshold are flagged for review.

\subsection{Delivery Policy}
\label{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy}

The delivery policy specifies how evaluation results affect development and
release decisions. Every delivery pipeline applies such a policy, even if only
through the checks it runs; CD4AI does not prescribe its rules. A policy may
use requirements for individual cases, thresholds across a suite, comparisons
against an accepted baseline, or defined review procedures performed by people
or AI agents. The policy should identify the conditions for deployment and the
action to take when those conditions are not met.

Whether a case blocks deployment is decided separately from why the case was
retained. A case that tracks a known limitation may still block release if it
enforces a critical requirement, while other limitations may be reported under
an explicit decision to defer their resolution. Such non-blocking results
remain visible and inform follow-up work; they do not change the expected
behavior or count as successful results.

Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} examines four case studies. For each, it
considers two aspects separately: whether it supports the feedback loop, and
how it connects evaluation results to delivery decisions, including how it
handles known limitations and potential regressions. One may support the first
without the second, for example by turning failures into evaluation cases
without specifying how their results affect delivery.

\section{Worked Example: From an Observed Failure to a Delivery Decision}
\label{sec:cd4ai-worked-example}
\label{sec:cd4ai-dynamics}

Suppose a customer-support assistant handles refund questions under a policy
that permits refunds within 30 days of purchase. The following hypothetical
workflow illustrates one way to implement CD4AI.
Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-case-lifecycle} summarizes how the example unfolds: an
incorrect answer observed during use, the evaluation case saved from it, and a
response from a proposed version. The case's input and expected behavior
remain the same as the application changes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-case-lifecycle}
  \caption{An incorrect refund answer becomes a reusable evaluation case.
    The robot represents the proposed version, whose illustrative answer is
    assessed against the saved expectation.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-case-lifecycle}
\end{figure}

During monitoring, an automated evaluator flags a conversation for review. The
customer asks, \enquote{I bought this 45 days ago. Can I get a refund?} The
assistant answers, \enquote{Yes, you can get a refund.} During curation, a
reviewer checks the policy and purchase details and confirms that the approval
is incorrect.

The reviewer then prepares an evaluation case from this conversation. Its input
is the customer's question, and its context includes the refund policy and the
purchase details. Its expected behavior is to explain that the purchase is
outside the refund window. An LLM judge assesses each answer against a rubric
that checks whether it correctly explains the customer's ineligibility, without
requiring a single exact phrasing. The case is added to the evaluation dataset
even though the defect is not yet fixed, making it available to track the
limitation and assess proposed fixes.

Running the case sends the question, with its stored policy and purchase
context, to the application version under test and passes the answer to the
judge. Because responses can vary across runs, the case is executed several
times on each version; one satisfactory answer alone does not confirm that an
intermittent defect is resolved. On the current version, these executions show
whether the case reproduces the incorrect approval and how often.

A developer then proposes a change to correct this behavior. The team runs the
saved case against the proposed version in the same way. Across the
repeated executions, its answers, such as \enquote{No. Your purchase is outside
the 30-day refund window}, meet the stated expectation, supporting the proposed
fix.

Before delivery, the proposed version also runs against the other cases in the
suite, and the results reveal an unintended side effect of the change. One case
asks about a purchase made 10 days ago and expects the
assistant to confirm the refund. The current version passes this case, but the
proposed version now denies the refund. Because the case passed before the
change, this failure is a potential regression. The team's delivery policy
requires established behavior to be preserved, so the change is blocked and
returned to the developer for investigation, even though it fixes the 45-day
answer. Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-delivery-decision} summarizes both results and
the resulting decision.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-delivery-decision}
  \caption{Evaluation results that inform the delivery decision. The proposed
    version fixes the original case but fails a case that the current version
    passes, so the delivery policy blocks the change.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-delivery-decision}
\end{figure}
