\chapter{The CD4AI Pattern}
\label{chap:cd4ai}

CD4AI is a continuous delivery pattern that addresses the following problem:
\enquote{How can teams turn failures observed during the use of AI-enabled
systems into evaluation cases that support subsequent changes?}
This chapter presents the pattern and its feedback loop.
Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} examines how its stages appear in selected
systems and documented workflows.

\section{Context, Problem, and Intent}
\label{sec:cd4ai-context}

% Context

Foundation models are trained on broad data at scale and can be adapted to
a range of downstream tasks~\cite{Bommasani:FoundationModels:2021}.
Applications built around language foundation models can change their behavior
through modifications to prompts, retrieved context, tools, and orchestration,
without necessarily retraining the underlying
model~\cite{Schluntz:BuildingEffectiveAgents:2024}.
By avoiding a model-training cycle for such changes, application teams can
shorten the implementation cycle and introduce behavioral changes within their
ordinary software development workflow.

In this setting, evaluating AI-dependent behavior becomes part of the
application team's delivery responsibilities, even when the team does not
develop or train the underlying model. A change that is quick to implement
may still require substantial evaluation. The resulting challenge is to assess
its effects on established behavior while incorporating failures discovered
during use into future evaluations.

% Problem

Initial evaluations cannot be assumed to cover every interaction that will
emerge during use. Teams therefore need a process for identifying candidate
failures, reviewing them, and turning confirmed defects into useful evaluation
cases. Collecting interactions alone does not establish this connection: the
cases must capture expected behavior and return to the evaluation suite used
to assess subsequent changes.

A further difficulty arises when a suite contains both known unresolved
limitations and behavior that the team expects to preserve. An undifferentiated
failure report does not establish whether a proposed change introduced a
regression or whether a known limitation persists. The evaluation process must
retain enough context to interpret the result and support a delivery decision.

% Intent

CD4AI aims to support frequent delivery while preserving established behavior
by connecting evaluation, monitoring, and curation in a recurring feedback loop.
Although applications built around language foundation models motivate the
discussion here, the pattern is described in terms of these responsibilities,
rather than a particular model or evaluation framework. Its intent is to make
observed failures inform future validation, without guaranteeing that every
failure will be discovered or that a previously observed defect will never recur.

Following the terminology in Section~\ref{sec:background-evaluation}, these
cases can document unresolved limitations, support confirmation of fixes, and
help detect regressions. The evaluation stage includes these purposes;
creating a case does not imply that the corresponding defect has been fixed.

\section{Overview of the Feedback Loop}
\label{sec:cd4ai-overview}

CD4AI uses failures found during use to improve the evaluations run before
future deployments. The team repeats three stages:

\begin{description}
  \item[Evaluation.] Run the existing evaluation cases against a proposed
    change. Apply the team's delivery policy to decide whether the results
    permit deployment or require further work.
  \item[Monitoring.] Observe the deployed application and collect interactions
    that may reveal failures for the team to investigate.
  \item[Curation.] Review these interactions to identify actual problems.
    Turn selected problems into evaluation cases that specify the expected
    behavior and how to check it.
\end{description}

Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-feedback-loop} summarizes the transitions between these
stages and the return of curated cases to evaluation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-feedback-loop}
  \caption{The CD4AI feedback loop. Evaluation provides evidence for delivery
    decisions; monitoring supplies candidate interactions for review;
    curation prepares evaluation cases for subsequent assessments.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-feedback-loop}
\end{figure}

The new cases are added to the evaluation suite and run against proposed fixes
and later changes, closing the loop. Failures discovered during use therefore
become part of what the team checks before future deployments.

Application changes, including defect fixes, are implemented through the team's
development process and assessed in the evaluation stage. Curation specifies cases
and expected behavior; it does not itself repair the application. The diagram
summarizes the flow of evaluation evidence rather than every development activity.

CD4AI requires an explicit policy for how evaluation results inform development
and delivery decisions. Each implementation defines which outcomes prevent
deployment and how other results inform tracking and improvement. Cases may
track known limitations or protect established behavior against regressions.
Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy} discusses how teams can define and
apply this policy.

The following example follows one case from the discovery of a problem to
regression protection. The subsequent sections explain how each stage works,
including how teams assess variable results, define the conditions for
deployment, and manage evaluation costs.

\subsection{Following a Failure through the Cycle}
\label{sec:cd4ai-dynamics}

Consider a hypothetical assistant that gives an incorrect answer about product
eligibility. Monitoring retains the interaction and flags it for review.
During curation, a reviewer confirms the defect, identifies the relevant policy,
and prepares a case describing the input, necessary context, and expected
behavior. The case is retained even if the team cannot immediately fix the
application. Its evaluation result makes the unresolved limitation visible.
The team's delivery policy determines whether that result blocks deployment
or whether resolution can be deferred while the limitation remains tracked.

When a developer proposes a change, the case helps assess whether the desired
behavior has been achieved. Other retained cases help assess the effects on
previously established behavior. If the fix is confirmed under the team's
evaluation criteria, the case can then be used to protect that behavior during
later changes. A subsequent failure calls for investigation of a possible
regression, taking execution and assessment variability into account.

In this hypothetical example, the input and expected behavior of the case
remain the same, but the reason for running it changes. Initially, it records
an unresolved limitation. When a fix is proposed, it helps check whether the
fix works. After the fix is confirmed, it helps detect whether later changes
reintroduce the problem.

\section{Monitoring: Finding Candidate Problems}
\label{sec:cd4ai-monitoring}

To achieve our objective of creating evaluations based on real production
problems, it is valuable to have an effective monitoring layer.
This section explains strategies that can be used to detect
potential defects in production.

\subsection{Selecting Candidates for Review}

Monitoring selects interactions for curation using signals of possible
problems, sampling, or a combination of both. Figure~\ref{fig:cd4ai-candidate-selection}
illustrates this selection and the handoff to curation.

A \emph{signal} is an observable indication that an interaction may contain a
problem and deserves review. Examples include negative user feedback and an
automated evaluator flagging an answer as unsupported by the retrieved
information~\parencite{MLflow:RetrievalGroundedness}. Neither confirms a defect:
feedback may express dissatisfaction with correct behavior, and the evaluator
may misjudge the answer. Signal design depends on the application; curation
determines whether a selected interaction reveals a defect.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{figures/cd4ai-candidate-selection}
  \caption{Candidate selection within monitoring. Solid arrows show the flow
    of interactions toward curation; dashed arrows indicate what guides
    selection.}
  \label{fig:cd4ai-candidate-selection}
\end{figure}

Precision and recall help describe selection
quality~\parencite[Section~8.3]{Manning:IR:2008}. For a defined population,
counting interactions rather than distinct defects:
\begin{gather*}
  \text{Precision} =
  \frac{\text{Number of selected problematic interactions}}
       {\text{Number of selected interactions}} \\[1ex]
  \text{Recall} =
  \frac{\text{Number of selected problematic interactions}}
       {\text{Number of problematic interactions}}
\end{gather*}
Low precision means a larger share of selected interactions consumes review
effort without revealing defects. Low recall means a larger share of problematic
interactions is missed. These measures help teams reason about selection
quality in relation to review capacity and application risks; CD4AI does not
require continuous measurement or prescribe numerical targets.

Execution cost also constrains selection. A team may run an expensive automated
assessment on a sample of interactions rather than on every interaction, trading
coverage for lower execution cost~\parencite{Databricks:ProductionMonitoring}.
Reviewing a sample of unflagged interactions can help investigate what the
signals miss. Finding
few defects in that sample does not necessarily indicate poor selection, since
its purpose is exploratory. Reviewing only flagged interactions cannot establish
the signals' recall: the number of problematic interactions left unflagged
remains unknown.

Consider a hypothetical customer-support assistant. Its monitoring process
forwards conversations with negative user feedback or answers flagged by an
automated evaluator to curation. Each day, it also selects a random sample of the remaining
conversations. Both sets enter the same review process: the signals direct
attention to suspected problems, while sampling explores interactions that
those signals did not flag. A sampled conversation might reveal an incorrect
answer that the evaluator did not flag and the user did not rate negatively.

The selected interactions are reviewed during curation, where they may become
evaluation cases. The following subsection discusses information that can
support this review.

\subsection{Observing Interactions}

We need to store enough information for a reviewer to reconstruct and assess
the relevant behavior. This includes the relevant context available to the
AI and the outputs it produced while performing its tasks. Examples include
inputs, conversation history, retrieved context, tool responses, outputs, and
tool calls.

If information is missing, curation may become more difficult because reviewers
may lack the context needed to assess the AI's decision. For example, if we
do not know which tool description an agent received, it may be hard to
assess whether the tool call was appropriate in that situation.

\section{Curation: Turning Observations into Evaluation Cases}
\label{sec:cd4ai-curation}

Curation reviews interactions selected during monitoring and prepares those
worth retaining as reusable evaluation cases. It establishes the expected
behavior and criteria for assessing it, so that observations from use can
inform subsequent evaluations.

\subsection{Reviewing Candidate Interactions}

Review determines whether the observed behavior met the relevant requirements
or expectations. A monitoring signal alone does not establish that a defect
occurred. People, automated assessment, or a combination of both may perform
this review. CD4AI
leaves that choice to the implementation; the judgment must have a basis in
what the application was expected to do.

Consider the hypothetical customer-support assistant introduced in the previous
section. An automated evaluator flags as unsupported an answer stating that a
customer is eligible for a refund. During curation, a reviewer checks the
refund policy and purchase details available to the assistant. If the policy
excludes that purchase and no exception applies, the answer reveals a defect.
If the available information supports the answer, the evaluator's flag was a
false alarm. If relevant purchase details are unavailable, the review may
remain inconclusive.

An inconclusive judgment does not establish that the behavior was acceptable.
The team may retain the interaction for further investigation without adding
it to the evaluation suite yet.

An interaction may also expose a situation the team had not anticipated.
Existing requirements may already determine the expected behavior; otherwise,
the team needs to clarify what the application should do before preparing an
evaluation case. Review can therefore reveal gaps in expectations as well as
failures to meet existing ones. Once the expected behavior is sufficiently
clear, the team can decide whether to retain the interaction as a reusable case.

\subsection{Preparing and Maintaining Evaluation Cases}

For an interaction selected for inclusion, curation prepares a reusable case
that specifies the input or scenario, relevant context, expected behavior,
and assessment criteria. Expected behavior need not be a single exact output:
for example, the assistant should explain that a purchase is ineligible for a
refund, while several phrasings may satisfy that requirement. Assessment may
use rule-based checks on structured outputs or an LLM acting as a judge that
assesses a response against a rubric~\parencite{Grace:DemystifyingEvals:2026}.
Section~\ref{sec:cd4ai-testing} discusses these methods and their limitations.

The case should preserve the relevant conditions under which the failure was
observed, including conversation history, retrieved information, or tool state
where needed. Omitting relevant context may produce an evaluation that no longer
exercises the observed problem. Preserving that context does not guarantee that
an intermittent failure will occur on every run; repeated execution and
reliability assessment belong to the evaluation stage.

Cases derived from observed failures complement those prepared from the
application's requirements and may be retained before the defects are fixed.
When adding a case, the team considers whether it contributes meaningful
coverage. Two interactions exposing the same defect may exercise different
conditions and justify separate cases. Conversely, if the relevant scenario
and expected behavior are already covered, an additional case may be redundant.
The decision depends on what the case contributes, not only on whether the
defect is already known.

When requirements change, the team should review affected cases and update
their expected behavior and assessment criteria where necessary. A change to
a product policy, for example, may change the expected answer to a customer's
request. Without these updates, evaluations may report failures even when the
application correctly follows the new requirements. Keeping a connection to
the original interaction or review can help explain why a case exists and
support its maintenance.

Prepared cases are made available to subsequent evaluations, for example
through a dataset or executable tests. This completes the connection from a
reviewed interaction to a reusable case. The next section discusses how cases
are exercised and how their results are interpreted.

\subsection{Known Limitations and Regression Protection}
\label{sec:cd4ai-result-interpretation}

% When developing this discussion, explain how the record of an unresolved
% limitation can be kept current without treating observed behavior as expected
% behavior. Keep case selection and expectation maintenance in curation.

A case documenting an unresolved defect can guide improvement; exercising it
against a proposed fix supports confirmation; retaining it after the behavior
is established supports regression protection. These are evaluation purposes,
not necessarily different data structures or mutually exclusive categories.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background-evaluation-purpose}, the purpose
of a case cannot be inferred solely from whether it currently passes or fails.

Interpreting a result requires the expected behavior, the application version
assessed, and the relevant reference evidence. That evidence may include a
reviewed record of the unresolved defect, results from an accepted baseline,
or explicit requirements for behavior that must be preserved. A continuing
failure should remain visible as a limitation; a change from previously
acceptable behavior should be investigated as potential deterioration.
Scores may also worsen while remaining below a pass threshold, so a comparison
should not rely exclusively on binary verdicts.

Where results are compared across versions, the cases, assessment criteria,
and execution conditions must support a meaningful comparison. New cases
without reference results and unsuccessful evaluation executions should be
identified separately. The implementation must also account for variability
in the application and its evaluators before attributing a difference to the
proposed change.

\subsection{Delivery Policy}
\label{sec:cd4ai-delivery-policy}

The delivery policy specifies how evaluation evidence affects development and
release decisions. CD4AI requires this connection to be explicit, while leaving
the decision rules and their enforcement to each implementation. A policy may
use requirements for individual cases, thresholds across a suite, comparisons
against an accepted baseline, or defined human review procedures. It should
identify the conditions for deployment and the action to take when those
conditions are not met.

A critical unmet requirement may block release even when the defect is already
known. Other unresolved limitations may be reported under an explicit decision
to defer their resolution. Such a decision does not change the expected behavior
or establish that the defect has been fixed.

Evaluation purpose and release requirements are separate decisions. A case
that tracks a known limitation may still enforce a critical requirement.
A case used for regression protection may allow variation within an explicitly
defined tolerance. Non-blocking results remain visible and inform follow-up
work; they are not equivalent to successful results. An evaluation whose result
does not block deployment may still be required to run and undergo review.

Teams may use separate suites, metadata, or filtered views to
distinguish evaluation purposes. Blocking and non-blocking outcomes describe
the effect of results under the policy, rather than two mandatory categories
of cases. A policy may require all assessed cases to pass or permit some failures
under stated conditions. The shared responsibility is to make the interpretation
and resulting decision explicit; exactly two datasets or a universal numerical
threshold are not requirements.

\noindent\emph{Working refinement.} The explicit delivery policy is proposed
here as a responsibility of the pattern. Chapter~\ref{chap:case-studies} will
examine how documented workflows connect evaluation results to decisions and
which mechanisms they provide for interpreting known limitations and potential
regressions. These questions will be examined separately from the evidence for
the three-stage feedback loop, without assuming a common division of cases.
